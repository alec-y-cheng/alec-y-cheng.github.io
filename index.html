Spring 2026—

Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulations is a mathematical approach to solving fluid flow problems, and while they are the closest known approximator of fluid simualtions, they are notorious for being computationally expensive, as a single simulation could take up to weeks to solve. Engineers and architects benefit from CFD when modeling wind in simulated urban plans as a way to predict pedestrian comfort on the ground level, yet often don't have this time to afford early in the ideation phase of their design process, so using CFD is almost always out of the question until much later in the design process.

Machine learning-based surrogate models have emerged as a strong alternative to predicting such wind flow, accurately approximating the solutions to complex CFD problems in no more than a second. The best candidate models vary tremendously in motivation and architecture, from CNNs in latent/fourier space to physics-informed transformer networks, constituting an exciting, rich field of research that can make complicated engineering and design practices much more accessible.

Image Classification and Scene Reconstruction

Machine Learning Computer Vision PyTorch Python

Fall 2025

Implemented an end-to-end pipeline of computer vision techniques to reconstruct 3D scenes from a mixed collection of unfiltered, crowdsourced 2D images. This pipeline used a CLIP model for image classification, followed by a comparison of 3 camera pose estimation methods, and finally gaussian splatting for the rendering. Experiments of these 3 types of camera pose estimation models, with classical COLMAP as a baseline, neural-network-based PoseNet, and a novel pose refinement model, revealed that PoseNet performed best (25% improvement) at the cost of long training time, while the simple neural network pose refinement model still succeeded in decreasing the error of COLMAP by 9% on average.

A 2.5D Approach to Training Robust 3D Pose Estimators in Specialized Execise Settings

Machine Learning Computer Vision PyTorch Python Academic Writing

Spring 2026

The biggest challenge in training robust 3D human pose estimators is that most large-scale annotated 3D datasets come from sterile, clean, lab environments that don't generalize well to diverse settings. This project investigates whether we can capitilize on the abundance and diversity of 2D datasets in training 3D pose estimators that perform well even in highly specialized domains.

This research project explored using either target or non-target-domain 2D data to improve the generalizability of the 3D pose estimator by subjecting the estimator to a 2D reprojection loss.

Results show that both using target-domain 2D pseudolabels or non-target 2D data indeed improved the generalizability of the model and succeded in reducing the loss of 3D human pose estimators significantly when evaluated on a gym exercise domain, and we believe it would work on other similarly specialized environments.

Neighborhood Toponymy Project

ArcGIS Architecture & Planning

Fall 2023

Project for GIS and Remote Systems class examines the relationship between median household income and neighborhood toponymy, the study of place names. This project examined 19 types of common place suffixes (e.g. containing the word "park", "heights", etc.) across 27 of the largest US cities (3721 neighborhoods in total), and the local and global patterns in income.

Using hot spot analysis and statistical tests on income aggregated on neighborhood name, no statistically significant correlation was found between neighborhood name and income. However, many local patterns hinted at a mediating role that geography plays in income differences that are present.

William Edmondson Homesite Project

ArcGIS

Summer 2024

The fight to preserve the pioneering African-American sculptor William Edmondson's legacy has been a decades-long fight between the city of Nashville, private developers, and the working-class Edgehill community he once called home. After his neighborhood's houses were cleared following Nashville's urban renewal, today an elementary school with a beloved memorial garden sits on the land, a space whose future has recently been put into question once again.

As a research assistant at the Vanderbilt Institute for Spatial Research, I georeferenced a catalog of historical housing, zoning, and aerial maps and site plans of Edmondson's Edgehill community. Understanding the spatial relationship between Edmonson's Edgehill and modern Edgehill can offer us clues into how the rooted Edgehill community has experienced and reacted to change amidst the boom and rebranding of one of America's fastest growing cities.

Campus Simulator Project

Modeling & Simulation Python ArcGIS Academic Writing

Spring 2026

Simulation of a large scale sporting event on Georgia Tech's campus. Involved in depth review of literature into modeling pedestrian flow. As opposed to macroscopic models like Hughes' model for pedestrian dynamics, we opted for an individual agent-based social model that represents agent movement motivation as a series of ordinary differential equations. We apply this logic to simulate the plausible traffic and congestion that would take place if a large sporting event took place on Georgia Tech's campus.

From Mystery to History

Machine Learning Computer Vision PyTorch Python Rhino/Grasshopper Architecture & Planning

Spring 2026

Designed for ImmerseGT26's historical preservation track sponsored by TanitXR, Mystery to History is a Snapchat XR Spectacles app that crowdsources public narratives, documents collective memory, and reimagines history.

Users are encouraged to photograph meaningful snapshots of their local public spaces. The application aggregates these images based on location, and as it accrues more users who photograph the same subjects and locations, a unified 3D model can iteratively be refined through stereogrammetry techniques. We can use this collective 3D model as a monument to what is considered meaningful to local communities, and begin to record a history where we document how these spaces change over time. In addition to crowdsourcing local history, the app uses machine learning models in point cloud completion to imagine what certain documented artifacts might have looked like prior to ruin, serving as an instructive tool in motivating people to inquire, document, and learn about the global and local past.

Rebranding Nashville Through its Most Famous Icon

Architecture & Planning Academic Writing

Spring 2024

The "I Believe in Nashville" mural is a seemingly shallow piece of street art, yet its extreme popularity in the media, within its local community, and among the American public, enable us to understand Nashville's relationship with the art and the city's charged past. In this thesis, I present three layers to view the mural. Beyond its surface understanding as a symbol of resilience in the face of disaster, it can also be seen as an insignia of "New Nashville," a city that exploits its own culture to outside developers at the expense of locals. And with the widespread digital circulation of the mural, we can understand it through the lens of a highly commercialized and divided society that uses the icon as a facade for unity.

This paper argues that the ways in which the public has received, replicated, parodied, and commodified “I Believe in Nashville” both reflects and perpetuates an especially Nashvilian tradition of falsifying its own identity to rebrand itself for growth.
