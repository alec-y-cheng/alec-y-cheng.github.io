1. Introduction

There are moments when words don't reach. On a document, a single 2D image can explain the scenario better than hundreds of words. Yet, there are situations where even 2D images prove insufficient. In such cases, a 3D scene can provide a more accurate, immersive, and efficient understanding of the situation than dozens of disconnected photographs.

Today, algorithms and methods have been wrapped into text-to-image tools that are widely available. Likewise, we wonder what is the efficient and effective way to generate a 3D scene based on 2D images?

Datasets

We have the simple STL-10 dataset [9] that was used in our initial image clustering implementation. When dealing with real data, we have a dataset from an Image Matching Challenge conducted by Google [11], which we are using to prototype our camera pos estimation algorithm. Finally, the Mip-NeRF 360 dataset [10] is being used for the initial investigation and development of our 3D scene reconstruction implementation.

At the moment, when all pieces are complete, we are thinking of using scenes from the Mip-NeRF dataset for all three parts. These will be handled in a pipeline fashion, where images are given to the image clustering algorithm, whose output will be used as input for camera pose estimation, which will in turn give both images from a single scene and their camera poses to the 3D scene reconstruction algorithm.

Throughout history, many historical sites and structures lost to natural disasters, wars, or reckless redevelopments, now exist only as fragments as photographs. Likewise, many critical moments such as accidents or crimes were captured by CCTV or bystanders in photographs from various angles, but none of these images alone can provide a "whole picture" needed for accurate reconstruction and investigation.

Some examples are the Buddhas of Bamiyan (image source [12]) and the Crystal Palace.

We are motivated by the possibility of using machine learning and computer vision to bridge the gap between 2D images and 3D scene:

  • To reconstruct disappeared historical sites in 3D (reviving them virtually from scattered photographic evidence)
  • To rebuild accident or crime scenes (generating a unified and spatially accurate 3D scene from multiple 2D sources)

3. Methods

3.1 Approach

We can break down the entire project into three separate tasks.

  • Image clustering
  • Develops and evaluates an unsupervised image clustering pipeline for grouping multi-view scene images.
  • Uses three pretrained visual backbones as frozen feature extractors:
  • ResNet-50 (supervised learning on ImageNet)
  • DINO ViT-S/16 (self-supervised learning)
  • CLIP ViT-B/32 (vision–language model, treated here as a supervised feature extractor)
  • Operates on a 15-scene subset of the Image Matching Challenge 2025 dataset.
  • Each scene (e.g., Taj Mahal, bike, peach tree farm) is stored in its own folder under 'my_data/' and serves as a ground-truth class for evaluation.
  • During clustering, labels are hidden; the models only see a pool of images and must partition them into 15 groups.
  • Extracts one high-dimensional feature vector (“fingerprint”) per image and L2-normalizes all features to enable cosine-similarity comparisons.
  • Applies PCA dimensionality reduction (original feature dimension → 50 components) to stabilize and accelerate clustering.
  • Applies K-Means clustering with k = 15 to assign every image to one of 15 clusters.
  • Evaluates clustering quality using Adjusted Rand Index (ARI), Normalized Mutual Information (NMI), and Silhouette Score for all three models.
  • Creates visualizations to analyze cluster quality:
  • t-SNE plots of the 2-D embeddings colored by predicted cluster.
  • 15×15 confusion matrices comparing true scene labels vs. predicted clusters.
  • For the best-performing model, writes clustered images into 15 new folders (c0 - c14) under 'my_data_output_*', producing clean, scene-consistent inputs for downstream camera-pose estimation and 3D reconstruction.
  • Camera pose estimation
  • Determines camera positions and orientations for each scene image via a rotation and translation matrix
  • Explores 3 techniques to camera pose estimation
  • COLMAP (traditional feature matching and reconstruction)
  • PoseNet (CNN-based pose regressor)
  • Pose Refiner (COLMAP + neural network refinement head)
  • Gaussian scene reconstruction
  • From the scene images and their camera poses, reconstruct the scene in 3D.
  • We will be using GSplat to reconstruct the scene.
  • While GSplat does come with some default methods, we have modified the Markov Chain Monte Carlo method detailed in [8].

While the output for each of these steps can feed into the next, we have datasets (and ground truth when applicable) that can be used as the input for any of these three.

3.2 Preprocessing

Image Clustering Preprocessing

Dataset Acquisition and Balancing — We construct the image-clustering dataset from the Image Matching Challenge 2025 Kaggle competition. From the original multi-view data, we curate 15 distinct scenes, each saved in its own directory under 'my_data/' (e.g., taj_mahal, bike, peach_tree_farm). All images in the same directory are treated as the same ground-truth class. This folder structure enables efficient loading with PyTorch while preserving the true scene labels for later evaluation (ARI, NMI, confusion matrices). During clustering, these labels are not used; the models operate in a fully unsupervised setting.

Image Normalization — Because each backbone has its own recommended preprocessing, we define three model-specific pipelines:

  • CLIP ViT-B/32: uses the open_clip preprocessing function, which resizes and center-crops images to 224×224 and normalizes them with CLIP’s mean and standard deviation.
  • ResNet-50: uses the ResNet50_Weights.IMAGENET1K_V2 transforms from torchvision, including resize, center crop to 224×224, conversion to tensor, and ImageNet normalization.
  • DINO ViT-S/16: resizes images so the shorter side is 256 pixels, center-crops to 224×224, converts to tensors, and normalizes with ImageNet mean [0.485, 0.456, 0.406] and std [0.229, 0.224, 0.225].

All images are loaded using a custom ImageFolderWithPaths dataset and a PyTorch DataLoader with batch size 32, shuffle=False, and multiple workers. This maintains a consistent ordering between images, ground-truth labels, and file paths while ensuring good GPU utilization.

Feature Dimensionality Reduction — Each backbone outputs a high-dimensional embedding for every image (e.g., 512-D for CLIP, 2048-D for ResNet-50, 384-D for DINO). We first L2-normalize each feature vector and then fit PCA with 50 components on the normalized feature matrix. The PCA projection preserves most of the variance and discriminative information while substantially reducing dimensionality, which improves the numerical stability and runtime of K-Means without noticeably degrading clustering performance.

Camera Pose Estimation Preprocessing

Quaternions, R, and t — COLMAP outputs images as quaternions (which can represent the same information as a rotation matrix in 4 values instead of 9), while GSplat expects rotation and translation matrices as input. PoseNet trains on quaternion poses, while much of our ground truth is in 3x3 rotation matrix form. Appropriate functions to convert between quaternion and 3x3 rotation matrices had to be implemented.

CSV and Texts — In order for our models to train on ground truth poses, we had to implement functions to convert CSV data into arrays to be passed into our models and apply the appropriate quaternion → R or R → quaternion transformations as needed.

Partitioning Datasets — Training data must be partitioned into training, validation, and testing sets appropriately to avoid data leakage. Most importantly, we had to decide what datasets we would be ultimately evaluating on to prevent our neural network based models from memorizing the testing data.

3D Scene Reconstruction Preprocessing

GSplat, which we will be using to reconstruct scenes in 3D, expects a cleaned dataset with camera poses associated with each image. All the previous steps constitute preprocessing for this last and final step.

3.3 Methodology

Image Clustering Methodology

Image clustering for scene reconstruction aims to group images into scene-consistent sets based on visual and semantic similarity. On the 15-scene Image Matching Challenge 2025 subset, our pipeline combines pretrained feature extractors, dimensionality reduction, and clustering:

  1. Pretrained Visual Backbones (CLIP / DINO / ResNet-50) — We treat three models as frozen feature extractors and compare their representations under the same downstream clustering procedure:
  • CLIP ViT-B/32 – a vision–language model trained contrastively on large-scale image–text pairs, producing 512-dimensional embeddings that capture high-level semantics.
  • DINO ViT-S/16 – a self-supervised vision transformer that learns 384-dimensional embeddings via knowledge-distillation objectives, emphasizing invariance to viewpoint and appearance changes without labels.
  • ResNet-50 – a convolutional network pretrained on ImageNet, from which we use the 2048-dimensional pooled feature vector from the penultimate layer.

For each model, we apply its preprocessing pipeline, pass all images through the backbone, and L2-normalize the resulting embeddings so that cosine similarity corresponds to the dot product. These normalized vectors act as unique “fingerprints” for each image.

  1. PCA (Principal Component Analysis)  — To obtain a compact and well-conditioned feature space, we fit PCA independently for each backbone on its normalized features and project them to 50 principal components. This common 50-D representation preserves most of the original variance while reducing noise and redundancy, which helps K-Means converge more reliably and quickly.
  2. K-Means Clustering — We run K-Means on the 50-D PCA features to partition all images into k = 15 clusters, corresponding to the number of curated scenes. We use 20 random initializations (n_init = 20) with a fixed random seed to mitigate sensitivity to initialization and approximate a good local minimum of the within-cluster variance objective. The resulting cluster assignments are then:
  • Compared with the hidden ground-truth scene labels using ARI, NMI, and Silhouette Score.
  • Visualized via t-SNE plots and 15×15 confusion matrices to qualitatively inspect how well each model’s feature space separates the scenes.

For the best-performing backbone (CLIP), we also export a folder-based view (c0–c14) of the clusters, which serves as the input dataset for the subsequent camera-pose estimation and 3D reconstruction components of the project.

Camera Pose Estimation Methodology

For camera pose estimation, we are exploring 3 approaches to pose estimation.

  1. COLMAP [3]  — a traditional SfM algorithm that uses SIFT feature extraction, matching, and homography for geometric verification to incrementally register images to a 3D model. This represented a state of the art reconstruction technique that has since been outperformed and replaced by neural network and transformer architectures. However, it is computationally inexpensive to run and is easily modularized for the purposes of our pipeline.

  1. PoseNet [4] — a model that takes in images as input and computes poses for images in the same scene. This is adapted from the GoogleNet architecture, and simply changes its classification head to a regressor, allowing it to predict values. It trains on labeled ground truth poses for image datasets, and processes one scene at a time.

  1. COLMAP with Neural Network Pose Refiner — an extension of COLMAP that takes in estimated COLMAP poses as input, and trains on ground truth images to compute a matrix that can best correct the COLMAP poses to make it closer to ground truth, the logic being that perhaps there are specific patterns in the COLMAP algorithm that consistently produce errors that can be learned and corrected. The architecture is a simple model consisting of 5 dense layers that learn a 6-DOF correction matrix that can be applied to the output of COLMAP, and then evaluated with a loss function that is derived from euclidean distance for translation and geodesic distance for rotation.

GSplat Methodology

For 3D scene reconstruction, GSplat [5] handles the rendering process, a point-based alpha-blending renderer introduced in [7]. This frees us to concentrate on training models. During training, an adaptive control step dynamically adjusts the number of gaussians in the scene, for which there are two well-known strategies [6] [8]. We will be experimenting with the second Gaussian Splatting Markov Chain Monte Carlo method, which we will call GS-MCMC.

  1. Levy-distributed Langevin Dynamics — The adaptive control strategy we modified uses a Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics method that was introduced in [18] and [19]. It is usually implemented with Gaussian noise, and in general terms, this noise is added to gaussian positions in each gradient descent step.

It is important to note this is only applied to transparent gaussians (the role of the sigmoid function). While solid, opaque gaussians contribute color to the scene, a number of gaussians may be optimized to be transparent, making them have no effect. These gaussians tend to be stationary, and are very unlikely to ever become opaque. The noise term is intended to move these gaussians to positions that aren’t yet correct, trusting the gradient steps to optimize them out of transparency.

While  is usually implemented as Gaussian noise (with , which GSplat’s implementation uses), recent research suggests this is not the most optimal way to implement SGLD [17]. In our case, this research makes some intuitive sense. If a gaussian is transparent, it is because its immediate vicinity has no loss, so it has been optimized to contribute no color where it is.

Moving it with gaussian noise is more likely to reposition it close to its current position, which is likely in an area with low loss. Alternatives have been presented in [15], [16], and [17] that make use of Levy alpha-stable distributions, which can be tuned to produce movements further away. We theorize this would increase the chance a transparent gaussian winds up in a space that happens to need more help (i.e. the position has higher error than its previous position).

We have opted to use the standard Levy distribution for our noise function. Since it only produces positive numbers, we also randomly assigned a sign to each number. The Levy distribution is defined with a few parameters, which become hyperparameters.

  • Location
  • The Levy distribution produces positive numbers starting at 0. This parameter can shift the curve horizontally.
  • Scale
  • Horizontal scaling of the distribution curve.
  • Percentage point function range
  • We use a percentage point function to create a noise value. In general terms, it accepts an input from (0.0, 1.0), and returns the value at that point of the distribution curve. For a normal distribution, ppf(0.5) would return the mean.

To be complete, the noise in the GS-MCMC strategy is further scaled by opacity.  The more transparent the gaussian, the more noise it gets. The default implementation uses a sigmoid function with a sharp cut-off value for opacity. We feel this scaling is appropriate.

  1. Adaptive Pixel-Loss-Based Repositioning — In addition to the above noise, GS-MCMC will periodically (every 100 epochs) force transparent gaussians to the positions of opaque gaussians. Candidate targets are randomly chosen based on their opacity, with more opaque gaussians being more commonly selected.

GSplat will store the 2D image space coordinates for each gaussian when they are rendered. Because we have these coordinates, we can find the total RGB loss at that gaussian’s 2D location. If that loss is high, then that indicates more help is needed in that area.

Our method assigns an initial weight of -log(1 - pixelL1Loss) to each gaussian, which influences the likelihood it is selected as a reposition target. The goal was to make it more likely that gaussians with high loss are selected in this repositioning step.

3.4 Evaluation Metrics

Image Clustering Error Metrics

To assess how well our unsupervised clusters recover the underlying scene structure, we use three complementary quantitative metrics plus a confusion-matrix visualization. Together, they capture both agreement with ground-truth labels and intrinsic cluster quality.

Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) — ARI measures the similarity between the predicted cluster assignments and the true scene labels by counting pairs of images that are consistently grouped together or separated in both partitions. It corrects the raw Rand Index for chance, producing values in [−1,1]: 1 indicates perfect agreement between clustering and ground truth. 0 corresponds to random cluster assignments. Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) — NMI quantifies how much information the predicted clusters share with the true labels. It is defined in terms of mutual information and entropy, and normalized to lie in [0,1]: 0 indicates no shared information (cluster assignments are independent of the labels). 1 indicates perfect correspondence between clusters and ground truth. The metric is defined as:

                                                         

The formula shown uses the mutual information I(C;K) between the clustering C and the ground-truth labels K, and their entropies H(C) and H(K). NMI is symmetric with respect to C and K and handles different numbers of clusters and classes gracefully.

Silhouette Score — Silhouette Score evaluates cluster quality without requiring ground-truth labels. For each image i, it compares: a(i): the average distance from i to all other samples in the same cluster (intra-cluster cohesion), and b(i): the smallest average distance from i to samples in any other cluster (inter-cluster separation).

                                                       

The silhouette coefficient s(i)s(i)s(i), shown in the equation, ranges from -1 to 1: (1) Values near 1 indicate that iii is well matched to its own cluster and far from others (dense, well-separated clusters). (2) Values near 0 indicate overlapping or ambiguous clusters. (3) Negative values suggest that the sample may be assigned to the wrong cluster.

The overall Silhouette Score is the mean of s(i)s(i)s(i) across all samples, providing a single intrinsic measure of cluster cohesion and separation.

Confusion Matrix Visualization — Although not a scalar error metric, the confusion matrix between true scene classes and predicted clusters provides valuable diagnostic information. Rows correspond to ground-truth classes and columns to predicted clusters. After optimally reordering cluster indices, strong diagonal entries indicate that each scene maps cleanly to a single cluster (good cluster–class alignment), whereas off-diagonal entries reveal scenes that are split across multiple clusters or merged together. This visualization helps interpret the quantitative scores and understand where different models succeed or fail.

Camera Pose Estimation Metrics

Camera Pose Precision — COLMAP uses a threshold of features seen across images to determine if it can register a new image to the existing model, which may lead it to detect outliers. We can evaluate how completely the model registers the same scene images in the same model with a precision metric.

Camera Pose Rotation and Translation Error with L2 Norm after Procrustes Algorithm — Assuming the same 3D world origin and coordinate system, the error between two sets of points can be computed by taking the L2 norm of their differences, or the Euclidean Distance between them.

However, Euclidean distance cannot be applied directly to rotation and translation matrices. In our case, we estimated the camera origin by reversing the operations of the transformation matrices themselves. If , then solving for camera coordinate = 0 gives us that the camera center lives at

The last issue we had to deal with when working between ground truth poses and estimated poses is that the world origin is not defined in either COLMAP or the ground truth label set, so even when the camera centers are obtained, they still cannot be meaningfully compared. We can subtract the centroid of each set of points from each set, such that they have the same origin, then we can apply Procrustes Algorithm [14] to find a transformation, which finds a single linear transformation that minimizes the squared distance between two sets of points. The final euclidean distance is normalized to the scene scale (norm of the camera distances from the coordinate) so that the value is comparable across scenes

Loss Functions — In the simple neural network pose refinement head we proposed, which takes COLMAP poses as output and outputs a rotation and translation correction matrix, a loss function is defined as the distance between the estimated COLMAP matrix after correction by the neural network head and the ground truth poses. Again, this deals with the differences in camera coordinates in the same way as described above. However, to directly compare rotation and translation matrices to each other, we cannot rely on comparing camera centers as we did when computing an error value. We use Geodesic distance [13]

 

as a metric to describe the loss between rotation matrices, and euclidean distance as the loss between translation vectors. The weighted sum is then returned as the overall loss function to minimize for the model.

GSplat Error Metrics

Commonly used metrics in Gaussian Splatting include a mix of performance metrics (computation time and memory), and various metrics for judging the similarity between two images. Typical comparison points are at 7000 epochs and 30000 epochs.

Peak Signal to Noise Ratio — One of the simplest metrics to track for any image processing algorithm, PSNR is simple to calculate based on the mean squared differences between two images (in this case, the image produced by Gaussian Splatting and ground truth).

Computation Time — Another simple, but practical measure that’s not too hard to record and is included in every Gaussian Splatting evaluation. Gaussian Splatting makes extensive use of CUDA kernels and tiled rendering techniques to improve the time spent in computation. Since Gaussian Splatting typically involves a fixed number of epochs to iterate over, we do not expect this to improve, though ideally it should not drastically increase.

Computation Memory — The memory usage of the algorithm, which can also be tracked throughout execution. Smaller memory footprints are better. Again, we do not expect our method to improve on this, but this should tell us if our modifications do not perform well.

Structural Similarity [20] — This is used in the traditional Gaussian Splatting loss function, but can also be a metric comparing the end result to ground truth. Structural similarity largely focuses on differences in brightness, contrast, and overall structure, and is meant to be closer to human perception than PSNR. Values range from 0 (not similar) to 1 (structurally equivalent). PyTorch has a built-in object for this which we will use.

Learned Perception Image Patch Similarity [21] — This is a relatively recent image similarity metric, which is used by both [6] and [8]. Like structural similarity, it is a metric that is meant to approximate human perception, and uses pre-trained neural networks to produce a similarity score. Smaller numbers are better, and PyTorch comes with objects which will calculate these.

Strengths and Limitations

Our experiments show that frozen, pretrained models plus a simple K-Means layer can perform effective unsupervised scene clustering:

  • CLIP features transfer well from large-scale image–text pretraining to our 15-scene dataset without task-specific fine-tuning.
  • The pipeline is modular: any model that outputs image features can be dropped into the same PCA + K-Means framework.
  • Writing clustered images into 15 output folders (c0–c14) produces an organized dataset that can be used directly for camera-pose estimation and 3D reconstruction.

Key limitations are:

  1. Fixed number of clusters. K-Means requires choosing k in advance (here k=15). For new datasets, we would need extra methods to estimate a good value of k.
  2. Appearance-only grouping. Clustering is based only on visual features, without geometric checks. Similar-looking but different scenes may be grouped, and the same scene under extreme changes may be split.
  3. Cluster shape assumption. K-Means works best when clusters are roughly spherical in feature space; elongated or multi-modal scenes may not be well captured.
  4. Dependence on pretrained models. Performance depends on what CLIP, ResNet, and DINO were trained on. Scenes that differ from their training distribution may be harder to separate.

Summary and Path Forward

On the Image Matching Challenge 2025 subset, our pipeline correctly recovers 15 real-world scenes in an unsupervised way, with CLIP giving the best results. The clustered folders (c0–c14) provide clean inputs for the next stages of our project: estimating camera poses and reconstructing 3D scenes with Gaussian splatting.

Next steps:

  1. Apply the pipeline to more datasets and connect it directly to COLMAP-, PoseNet-, and GSplat-based reconstruction modules.
  2. Combine appearance and geometry, for example by using camera metadata or feature matching, to further reduce scene confusion.
  3. Explore alternative clustering methods that do not require a fixed k and can better model complex cluster shapes.

Overall, this work provides a practical foundation for a full pipeline that moves from unsupervised scene clustering to camera-pose estimation and finally to 3D reconstruction.

Discussion and Future Work — In this section, we explored three methods to estimate camera poses for 3D reconstruction. COLMAP, while an older, traditional method, is still widely used today for its low computational burden and robust results, often falls short when it comes to sparse scenes or cameras with widely varying intrinsics. Our proposed adaptation of COLMAP with a neural network, attempts to identify any numerical patterns in how COLMAP fails to estimate poses correctly, and the results show that there is marginal improvement in error after applying this head. Finally, PoseNet showed that a fully neural network based approach was the best estimator of camera pose, but that this model, too, has the same shortcomings when it comes to sparse datasets. The main tradeoff is that this model needed a great deal of training to outperform COLMAP on test data.

In the future, work can be done on the COLMAP pipeline to try to correct when the algorithm misclassifies scenes with sparse images, although any modifications would have to be much more involved than simply adding a head to the end of COLMAP. With such an improved algorithm, the 3D reconstruction stage would have more data to work with, leading to a stronger reconstruction.

GSplat Results, Discussion

Because of limitations in execution time, each set of hyperparameters for each of our three modifications were tuned in isolation, in order to reduce the number of combinations we had to try. We used K-folds with 4 folds for tuning, and initialized the scene from random points to start with maximum error (as opposed to starting with some known set of SfM points).

This allowed us to perform an ablation study to see how each of our three modifications performed on their own. We discuss these below.

Execution Time — During hyperparameter tuning, we discovered that our implementation ran much slower than the original. Most GS evaluation is done at 7000 and 30000 epochs, though tuning parameters for that amount of time was prohibitively slow. We instead tuned our hyperparameters for 5000 epochs, then ran a final evaluation at 7000 epochs.

We only experienced this slowdown when tuning the Levy distribution parameters, so the cause of this was the PRNG. PyTorch does not come with native support for the Levy distribution, so our two options were either SciPy or Pyro, a PyTorch library. Despite being coded using PyTorch functions, Pyro was no faster than SciPy, so we ended up using SciPy.

If our approach is evaluated on accuracy per epoch, however, this allows our method to be compared assuming we are not limited by the speed of our PRNG.

Memory Usage — Individually and combined, none of the three modifications we did increased memory usage by any significant amount. We perform as well as the original for this metric.

Levy-distributed Langevin Dynamics — We found the Levy distribution to produce better results with the right hyperparameters. This comes at a severe cost of execution speed, but all three accuracy metrics dramatically increased for our testing scene.

Discussion and Future Work — At higher epochs, we actually do expect the two methods to converge to around the same accuracy values as they both approach an acceptable solution. This is a natural part of training in general, given two models that produce good results.

If there are other reasons this could be the case, it’s possible our method works best when there’s currently a lot of error. Our method prefers to push transparent gaussians away from their current area (which is why we used the Levy distribution), rather than keeping them close (as the normal distribution prefers). It’s possible that our more aggressive strategy works best when there’s currently a lot of error. As the reconstruction approaches its end and needs only minor adjustments, it’s possible the more centered approach of the normal distribution is more suitable.

Also, due to our short development cycle, the hyperparameters were pretty much tuned on a single scene, and for a small number of epochs, then briefly tested on others to see if it produced a generalizable solution. While initial signs show that it does generalize, a full investigation would require more thorough testing and tuning on many scenes and higher epoch values. We’ve already discussed the limitations we were working with, however.

Execution time was the biggest barrier to our experiments, and in practical terms, one could run the original, much faster GS-MCMC model for more epochs to achieve similar results. However, it may be possible to use a normal distribution and transform it into a distribution that looks similar to the Levy distribution, or at least a distribution that shares the same concept of pushing points out further. This would retain the normal distribution’s speed advantage, and combine it with a faster way of testing our hypotheses.

Our metrics were run using the counter scene from the Mip-NeRF 360 dataset. This dataset is rather well-behaved - the scene is static (i.e. there are no changes in the scene, though it’s taken from several angles), the images are generally centered in the same spot, and lighting conditions are constant. 4.1 Stress Test

As a final test, we ran both the original GS-MCMC algorithm and our implementation through an image set of the Taj Mahal. These images were taken from the output of our image clustering algorithm, and the camera poses were extracted using our refined COLMAP implementation.

It should be noted this image set is extremely non-ideal. Different people are cluttering up the scene in each shot, some shots are closer or center on different parts of the Taj Mahal, and they are all taken in different lighting conditions, which adds quite a lot of noise to the dataset. One image was even taken from a tunnel, with much of the picture black except for the Taj Mahal peeking out at the end of the tunnel.

This image set is the sort that Gaussian Splatting will struggle on, and this is exactly what we noticed with the original GS-MCMC algorithm at 7000 epochs. It actually crashed at around 16000, taking an uncharacteristic 13.87 GB of memory.

We then compared this to our implementation, and noticed our accuracy metrics were still higher on average. We only ran 7000 epochs, and expect at higher epochs it will also run out of memory. The original GS-MCMC method had a range of results, at best matching our method, but usually producing worse results. By contrast, our method did not have much variance.
